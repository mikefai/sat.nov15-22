## 3. Reading & Writing Teaching Lessons

### Lesson 3.1: Understanding Dense Academic Prose — Claim Architecture, Evidence vs. Interpretation, and Disciplined Inferences

International students often read every sentence of a science or history passage at the **same flat volume**—treating every sentence as equally important. Native academic writers do **not** write flat paragraphs: they organize 90-word SAT passages around a tight **3-move hierarchy**.

#### A. Separating the Author's Claim from Background Views & Raw Evidence
Every academic passage distinguishes three layers—never confuse them!
1. **Old / Conventional View (`Not the Author's Claim!`):** Signaled by *Traditionally, Historians long assumed, Conventional models posit, Early observers attributed X to Y.*
2. **Empirical Evidence / Observation (`Raw Facts`):** Specific measurements, dates, chemical ratios, experiment outcomes, or archival records (*"Isotope ratios in tooth enamel varied by 14%," "Subjects took 42 ms longer"*). Raw facts cannot be argued with—they are the **data**.
3. **Author's / Researcher's Claim (`Interpretation of the Data`):** The conclusion drawn *from* the facts, signaled by pivot verbs: *suggesting that, indicating that, demonstrating that, yet these findings underscore, researchers therefore contend.*

> **Golden Rule for `Central Ideas` & `Details`:**  
> An answer choice that accurately summarizes Sentence 1's *old belief* as if the author still believes it is the **#1 Old-View Reversal Trap**. Always locate the **final pivot (`however, yet, actually, instead`)**—the author's true thesis lives *after* the pivot!

---

#### B. How to Solve Digital SAT `Inferences` Like Mathematical Deductions
An SAT `Inferences` passage ends with a blank (`..., suggesting that ______` or `Therefore, ______`). Treat the passage like two or three puzzle pieces on a table:
- **Step 1 — List the Stated Premises (`P1`, `P2`, `P3`) in 5-Word Notes:**
  - *Example:* `P1: Chemical W blocks root growth in barley.` `P2: Soil microbe M breaks down Chemical W into harmless nitrogen.` `P3: Sterilized soil kills Microbe M.`
- **Step 2 — Ask "What Must Happen When Premises Combine?"**
  - Combine `P1 + P2 + P3`: In sterilized soil exposed to Chemical W, barley roots will be stunted compared to unsterilized soil containing Microbe M!
- **Step 3 — Run the Four-Trap Elimination Filter:**
  1. **Too Extreme / Absolute:** Uses *always, exclusively, never, only, impossible, completely* when premises show *partial* or *conditional* effects.
  2. **Causal Reversal:** Flips `A causes B` into `B causes A`.
  3. **Alien Comparison:** Introduces `"more important than," "faster than other species,"` or `"cheaper than"` when no second species or cost was ever mentioned!
  4. **Real-World Guess:** Sounds smart from biology class, but uses zero constraints from `P1 + P2`.

#### Worked Example 3.1 (Separating Observation from Inference)
> *Original SAT-Style Example:*  
> Nineteenth-century Venetian glassmakers guarded cobalt-blue furnace recipes through guild secrecy laws that forbade master artisans from emigrating. Chemical spectroscopy of seventeenth-century blue goblets blown in Amsterdam workshops, however, reveals the exact trace bismuth-cobalt impurity ratio unique to ore mined in Saxony for Venetian guild furnaces, and municipal marriage registries in Amsterdam list three glassblowers born on the Venetian island of Murano, implying that \_\_\_\_\_\_
>
> A) Dutch glassmakers independently discovered how to synthesize bismuth-cobalt ore in local Amsterdam laboratories.  
> B) Venetian guild secrecy laws did not completely prevent either specialized raw materials or skilled artisans from reaching rival glassmaking centers.  
> C) seventeenth-century Amsterdam goblets commanded higher auction prices than goblets blown on Murano.  
> D) Saxony mines exported bismuth-cobalt ore exclusively to Amsterdam after 1650.  

- **Premise Mapping:**
  - `P1:` Venice passed laws forbidding masters from emigrating to guard recipes.
  - `P2 (however!):` Amsterdam goblets contain Venice's exact Saxony ore signature AND Amsterdam marriage rolls list three Murano-born glassblowers!
- **Evaluate Choices:**
  - **A** contradicts `"ore mined in Saxony"`.
  - **B** connects `P1` + `P2` with hedged precision (*"did not completely prevent either... materials or... artisans"*).
  - **C** invents *auction prices* (Alien Comparison!).
  - **D** uses extreme *"exclusively"* (contradicts Venice using Saxony ore).
  - **Correct: B.**

### Lesson 3.2: Locating Textual & Quantitative Proof (`Command of Evidence`)

Every right answer on the Digital SAT is **provable on the page**. On `Command of Evidence` questions, the passage hands you the claim for free—your job is to find the **one quote or data comparison that proves EVERY required piece of that claim**.

#### A. The "Two-Half Checklist" for Textual Evidence (Science & Literature)
Most SAT hypotheses have **two linked conditions**:
$$\text{Claim: } \underbrace{\text{Mechanism / Condition X}}_{\text{Half 1}} \longrightarrow \underbrace{\text{Produces Specific Outcome Y}}_{\text{Half 2}}$$
- **Trap Type A (`Half-Right Quote`):** Mentions Condition X vividly, but says nothing about Outcome Y!
- **Trap Type B (`Opposite Polarity Quote`):** Mentions both X and Y, but says X *failed* to produce Y!
- **Literary / Poetry Evidence Tip for Non-Native Speakers:** Do **not** panic over poetic metaphors! Translate concrete images into abstract emotional/thematic equivalents:
  - *Storms, frayed ropes, flickering lamps* $\rightarrow$ instability / uncertainty / mortality.
  - *Stone vaults, amber, locked chests* $\rightarrow$ permanence / preservation / secrecy.
  - Match both halves of the critic's thesis directly to the image's action!

#### B. Quantitative Evidence (Tables & Graphs) — 4-Step Proof Protocol
1. **Box the Exact Thesis in the Final Sentence of the Text** (before looking at numbers!).
2. **Check Table Headers, Units, & Denominators (5 Seconds):** Watch out for *rates per km²* vs. *raw counts*, or *percent lost* vs. *percent remaining*!
3. **Verify Data Truth First (`True vs. False to Table`):** Cross out any choice that misreads a row/column number. *(Usually eliminates 1–2 choices in 10 seconds!)*
4. **Verify Logical Sufficiency (`Does This True Fact Prove the Boxed Thesis?`):** Eliminate choices that state 100% accurate numbers from the table that belong to the wrong group or omit the crucial baseline comparison!

#### Short Comprehension Check — Lesson 3.2
- **`CC-D3-RW3`:** Suppose researchers claim: *"Adding biochar to sandy soil increases tomato yield **only when** paired with compost."* A table shows: `Sandy Soil = 4 kg`, `Biochar Only = 4.1 kg`, `Compost Only = 6 kg`, `Biochar + Compost = 9.5 kg`. Why is citing *"Biochar + Compost produced 9.5 kg compared to 4 kg for Sandy Soil"* **insufficient by itself** to prove `"only when paired with compost"`? Which extra number from the table is required?

### Lesson 3.3: Text Structure, Underlined-Sentence Function, and Cross-Text Connections

#### A. Function of an Underlined Sentence (`"Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?"`)
Never judge an underlined sentence in isolation! Always read **1 sentence BEFORE** and **1 sentence AFTER**, then classify its structural job:
- **Opening Sentence Underlined:** Usually *introduces a conventional view that the text later challenges*, *frames a scientific puzzle*, or *states the central claim*.
- **Middle Sentence with `However / Yet` Underlined:** Usually *presents empirical evidence that complicates/undermines the preceding assumption* or *introduces a methodological limitation (`caveat`)*.
- **Middle Sentence with `For instance / Specifically` Underlined:** *Provides a concrete illustration supporting the preceding generalization*.
- **Final Sentence Underlined:** *Draws a broader implication*, *states an unresolved question for future research*, or *qualifies the scope of the finding*.

#### B. Cross-Text Connections — The 3-Line Scratchpad Map
Read **Text 1**, write 5 words (`T1: Cause = X`). Read **Text 2**, write 5 words + relationship symbol (`T2: Grants X locally, but Cause = Y earlier [±]`).
- **90% of Correct Cross-Text Answers on Hard Questions** describe **qualified disagreement (`±`)**:
  - *"Acknowledge X, but argue that X is insufficient to explain..."*
  - *"Agree that Trend T exists, while attributing T to a different mechanism..."*
  - *"Point out a methodological caveat that limits how broadly Text 1's conclusion can be extrapolated..."*

#### Short Comprehension Check — Lesson 3.3
- **`CC-D3-RW4`:** Sentence 1 states a broad rule about bird migration. **Sentence 2 (underlined)** begins *"Arctic terns, however, depart three weeks before daylight shortens..."* Sentence 3 explains they track plankton blooms instead. What is the exact structural function of Sentence 2?
- **`CC-D3-RW5`:** On a Cross-Text question, Text 2 begins: *"While Dr. Kim rightly notes that urban tree canopies lower sidewalk temperatures..."* Why can you immediately eliminate an answer choice starting with *"By rejecting Dr. Kim's claim that urban trees cool sidewalks"*?

#### 21. **posit** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To put forward a hypothesis, model, or assumption as a starting point for scientific or philosophical reasoning.
- **Usage Register:** Almost exclusively academic! Neutral tone—neither proves nor disproves the idea; simply introduces a proposed model (`"Classical economics posits that..."`).
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** hypothesize, postulate, propose · **Ant:** prove conclusively, refute, dismiss.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"To explain why Mars displays dry river valleys despite faint early sunlight, atmospheric chemists **posited** that transient bursts of volcanic hydrogen warmed the young planet."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Scientists proposed a testable theoretical model (*volcanic hydrogen warming*) to account for the dry river valleys.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Thinking *posit* means *"positive / optimistic"* because of spelling! A scientist can *posit* a catastrophic extinction model—it simply means **propose a hypothesis**.
- **Short Application Activity:** Replace bracketed words: *"Early linguists [proposed the hypothesis] that syntax evolved from gesture."* (`posited`).

#### 22. **entail** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To involve or require something as an unavoidable, necessary part or logical consequence.
- **Logical Precision:** In logic and SAT Inferences, `A entails B` means **you cannot have A without B**!
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** necessitate, require, involve · **Ant:** exclude, preclude, rule out.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"Converting a nineteenth-century brick warehouse into a seismic-grade rare-book archive **entails** reinforcing timber floor joists and installing precision humidity locks."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** You cannot convert the warehouse into a safe archive without necessarily performing structural reinforcement and humidity control.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Guessing *entail* has something to do with an animal's *tail* ("coming at the end") or *"telling a story/tale."* Focus on **require necessarily**!
- **Short Application Activity:** If deep-sea mining **entails** disturbing slow-growing manganese nodules, can deep-sea mining happen *without* disturbing them? (`No—entails means unavoidable consequence!`)

#### 23. **empirical** *(adjective)*
- **Simple English Definition:** Based on real-world observation, experiment, or measurable data rather than pure logic, theory, or opinion.
- **Related Word Family:** *empirically* (adv. — `"empirically verified"`), *empiricism* (n.).
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** observational, experimental, data-driven · **Ant:** purely theoretical, speculative, conjectural, anecdotal.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"Although mathematical models predicted smooth airflow over the turbine blade, **empirical** wind-tunnel tests revealed unexpected vortex shedding along the trailing edge."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** `Although` contrasts *abstract math equations on paper* with *actual physical wind-tunnel measurements* (*empirical* tests).
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Confusing **empirical** ("based on experimental/observed data") with **imperial** ("relating to an empire or emperor," e.g., *imperial Rome*)! Check the first two letters: **`em-`** = experiment/observation; **`im-`** = empire!
- **Short Application Activity:** Pick `empirical` or `imperial`: *"Field surveys of 400 tide pools provided \_\_\_\_\_\_ support for the predation model, whereas \_\_\_\_\_\_ decrees regulated salt taxes in Tang China."* (`empirical` / `imperial`).

#### 24. **extrapolate** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To estimate or project unknown values/trends beyond your existing data by assuming current patterns continue.
- **Related Word Family:** *extrapolation* (n.). On the SAT, often appears in methodological critiques (*"cautioned against extrapolating from lab mice to wild populations"*).
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** project, extend, generalize · **Ant:** measure directly, restrict to observed sample.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"Demographers warned against **extrapolating** century-long national population forecasts from a five-year postwar baby boom."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Stretching a short 5-year spike 100 years into the future assumes an unusual temporary trend will last forever (*extrapolating* recklessly).
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Treating *extrapolate* as identical to *infer*. **Infer** draws a tight conclusion *inside* what the evidence warrants; **extrapolate** projects *outside* the measured range!
- **Short Application Activity:** Connecting to Math Lesson 1.4: Why can't you **extrapolate** a dorm-student shuttle survey (`MATH-D1-Q11`) to commuter students? (`Commuters lie outside the sampled range/conditions!`)

#### 25. **refute** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To prove a claim, accusation, or theory **decisively wrong** using hard evidence or airtight logic.
- **CRITICAL SAT DISTINCTION (`refute` vs. `dispute/reject`):** In casual speech people misuse *refute* to mean "say no." In formal SAT English, **dispute** = argue against (maybe without proof), whereas **refute** = **successfully disprove with evidence**!
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** disprove, debunk, invalidate · **Ant:** *substantiate*, *corroborate*, *vindicate*.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"By demonstrating that sterilized nutrient broth remained microbe-free inside swan-neck flasks for years, Louis Pasteur decisively **refuted** the doctrine of spontaneous generation."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Pasteur didn't merely express doubt—his controlled flask experiment *proved spontaneous generation false* (*refuted* it).
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Picking *"Text 2 refutes Text 1"* on a Cross-Text question when Text 2 merely *questions* or *qualifies* Text 1 (`±`). Remember: *refute* is a **100% knockout disprove**!
- **Short Application Activity:** Scale from weakest pushback (`1`) to strongest disproof (`3`): *refute*, *qualify*, *undermine*. (`1 = qualify [limit scope]`, `2 = undermine [weaken]`, `3 = refute [prove completely false]`).

#### 26. **underscore** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To emphasize, highlight, or draw strong attention to an important fact or conclusion.
- **Visual Memory Hook:** Literally *"draw a line (`score`) under (`under`) words on a page to highlight them!"*
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** emphasize, highlight, stress, accentuate · **Ant:** downplay, minimize, obscure *(and NEVER confuse with **undermine**!)*.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"The sudden collapse of three genetically uniform banana plantations during a single fungal outbreak **underscored** the ecological vulnerability of monoculture farming."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** The plantation disaster vividly highlighted and emphasized (*underscored*) how risky genetic uniformity is.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** **#1 High-Cost Trap on SAT R&W:** Seeing `under-` at the front of **underscore** and reading it as **undermine** ("weaken")! Drill this forever: **undermine = weaken (`-`)**; **underscore = emphasize (`+`)**!
- **Short Application Activity:** Choose `underscores` or `undermines`: *"Finding microplastics in pristine Himalayan snow \_\_\_\_\_\_ how far atmospheric currents transport synthetic fibers."* (`underscores` — emphasizes!).

#### 27. **delineate** *(verb)*
- **Simple English Definition:** To describe, map, or mark the exact boundaries, stages, or features of something clearly and precisely.
- **Root Clue:** Notice **`line`** right inside `de-LINE-ate` ("draw the exact outline/boundary lines around")!
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** outline, define, trace, demarcate · **Ant:** blur, muddle, conflate.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"In her treatise on maritime jurisdiction, the legal scholar carefully **delineated** the boundary between coastal territorial waters and international high-seas shipping lanes."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Legal precision requires drawing clear, unambiguous lines (*delineating*) between national and international waters.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Guessing `de-` means "delete / erase lines." Here `de- + line` means **trace out completely in clear detail** (often seen in `Text Purpose` choices: *"To delineate the stages of..."*).
- **Short Application Activity:** If a biology paragraph *"delineates the metabolic roles of two enzymes,"* what is the paragraph doing? (`Clearly outlining/distinguishing what each enzyme does.`)

#### 28. **tenuous** *(adjective)*
- **Simple English Definition:** Very weak, thin, slight, or poorly supported by evidence.
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** flimsy, fragile, shaky, slender, dubious · **Ant:** *compelling* (Day 2 #13!), robust, substantial, airtight.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"Because the two dialects share fewer than six basic vocabulary roots—a count easily explained by chance borrowing—linguists regard claims of a common ancestral family as **tenuous**."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Fewer than six shared words that could just be coincidence makes the family-tree claim extremely weak and flimsy (*tenuous*).
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Confusing **tenuous** ("weak/flimsy") with **tenacious** ("stubbornly persistent / holding on tightly") or **strenuous** ("physically exhausting")!
- **Short Application Activity:** Pair opposites: Strong evidence is **compelling**; weak, flimsy evidence is **\_\_\_\_\_\_**. (`tenuous`).

#### 29. **attribute** *(verb: uh-TRIB-yoot / noun: AT-trih-byoot)*
- **Simple English Definition:**
  - *(verb — `attribute X to Y`):* To regard `X` as caused by, produced by, or written/painted by `Y`.
  - *(noun — `an attribute`):* A characteristic quality or feature of someone/something.
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn (v.):** ascribe, credit, trace to · **Syn (n.):** trait, property, characteristic.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"Whereas nineteenth-century physicians **attributed** high-altitude fatigue to toxic mountain minerals, French physiologist Paul Bert demonstrated that lower barometric oxygen pressure was responsible."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** Notice the exact grammar `attributed [Effect: fatigue] to [Proposed Cause: toxic minerals]`.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Flipping cause and effect in `attribute X to Y`! Remember: **`X` is the result/painting; `Y` (after `to`) is the cause/creator**!
- **Short Application Activity:** In *"Curators attributed the bronze bust to Donatello,"* which is the artwork and who is the sculptor? (`Bust = artwork; Donatello = creator/source`).

#### 30. **caveat** *(noun)*
- **Simple English Definition:** An important warning, qualification, or limiting condition that stops people from over-interpreting a statement or result.
- **Latin Origin Used Daily in English Science:** Literally *"let a person beware"* — standard English academic noun (`"added an important caveat"`, `"with one methodological caveat"`).
- **Synonym / Antonym:** **Syn:** qualification, caution, proviso, limitation · **Ant:** unqualified endorsement, sweeping guarantee.
- **Original SAT-Style Contextual Sentence:** *"The clinical trial reported promising cholesterol reductions, though the authors highlighted a major **caveat**: all participants were marathon runners under age thirty-five."*
- **Explanation of Contextual Meaning:** After the colon comes a serious warning/limitation (*only young athletes were tested*), preventing doctors from assuming older patients will respond identically.
- **Common International-Student Misunderstanding:** Confusing *caveat* with *cavity / cave* ("hole in rock/tooth")! In SAT reading, a **caveat** is a **limiting caution attached to a claim**.
- **Short Application Activity:** Connect Day 1 + Day 3: When a scientist adds a **caveat** to her conclusion, is she **amplifying** or **qualifying** (`Day 1 RW-Q3`) her claim? (`Qualifying [limiting] her claim!`)

## 6. Math Teaching Lessons

### Lesson 3.4: Priority Advanced Math I — Quadratics, Function Transformations, Polynomial Identities & Radical Equations

#### A. Quadratic Master Toolkit (Faster Than Brute-Force Expanding!)
For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a \neq 0$):
1. **Sum & Product of Solutions (Vieta's Shortcuts — 3-Second Lifesavers!):**
   $$\text{Sum of solutions } (r_1 + r_2) = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad \text{Product of solutions } (r_1 \cdot r_2) = \frac{c}{a}$$
   *(Warning: First glance at $D = b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$ if the question specifies "real solutions"!)*
2. **Function Graph Transformations ($g(x) = f(x - h) + k$):**
   - **Inside parentheses ($x - h$) moves HORIZONTALLY OPPOSITE the sign!**  
     $f(x - 5)$ shifts the graph **RIGHT $5$ units** (every feature's $x$-coordinate increases by $+5$: vertex $(-2, -9) \to (3, -9)$!).  
     $f(x + 5)$ shifts the graph **LEFT $5$ units**.
   - **Outside parentheses ($+ k$) moves VERTICALLY WITH the sign!**  
     $+ 4$ shifts **UP $4$ units**; $- 4$ shifts **DOWN $4$ units**.
3. **Polynomial Identity / Coefficient Matching (`"true for all values of x"`):**
   Whenever $(ax + 3)(2x - b) = 10x^2 - 29x - 21$ holds *for all $x$*, match like powers on both sides:
   - $x^2$ terms: $2a = 10 \implies a = 5$.
   - Constant terms: $-3b = -21 \implies b = 7$.
   - Always check the middle $x$ term (`-35x + 6x = -29x` ✓) to catch sign traps!

#### B. Rational Common Denominators & Radical Extraneous Roots
- **Adding Algebraic Fractions:** Never add denominators! $\frac{A}{x - 3} + \frac{B}{x + 3} = \frac{A(x + 3) + B(x - 3)}{(x - 3)(x + 3)} = \frac{A(x + 3) + B(x - 3)}{x^2 - 9}$. *(Or graph the original expression and choices A–D in Desmos to match curves instantly!)*
- **#1 Radical Equation Trap (`Extraneous Solutions`):**  
  In mathematics, the radical symbol $\sqrt{\text{expression}}$ denotes the **principal (non-negative) square root** ($\sqrt{9} = +3$, **never** $-3$!). Squaring both sides of $\sqrt{4x + 21} = x$ destroys negative-sign information and invents fake answers where $x < 0$!  
  **Non-Negotiable Rule:** **ALWAYS plug every candidate root back into the original un-squared radical equation** (or graph `y = sqrt(4x + 21)` and `y = x` in Desmos—Desmos ONLY draws the true principal root!).

### Lesson 3.5: Problem-Solving and Data Analysis — Squared Units, Percent Wording, Residuals, Conditional Probability & Study Design

`Problem-Solving and Data Analysis` (~15% of SAT Math) is the **most English-heavy Math domain**—international students rarely miss the arithmetic, but lose points to **five English-to-Math translation traps**:

#### 1. Squared & Cubed Unit Conversions ($1\text{ km}^2 \neq 1{,}000\text{ m}^2$!)
Always **square the linear conversion factor** when converting area (and cube it for volume!):
$$1\text{ km} = 1{,}000\text{ m} \implies 1\text{ km}^2 = (1{,}000\text{ m})^2 = 1{,}000{,}000\text{ m}^2$$
$$1\text{ foot} = 12\text{ inches} \implies 1\text{ ft}^2 = 144\text{ in}^2, \quad 1\text{ ft}^3 = 1{,}728\text{ in}^3$$

#### 2. English Percent Wording Precision
- **"$A$ is what percent of $B$?"** $\implies \frac{A}{B} \times 100\%$.
- **"$A$ is what percent GREATER THAN $B$?"** $\implies \frac{A - B}{\mathbf{B}} \times 100\%$ *(Denominator is ALWAYS the noun right after **"than"** or **"of"**!)*.
- **Reverse Percent ("After a $20\%$ discount, price is $\$640$"):** Never add $20\%$ of $\$640$! Set $0.80P = 640 \implies P = \frac{640}{0.80} = 800$.

#### 3. Scatterplots & Residuals
- **Line of Best Fit ($\hat{y}$):** Smooth model prediction; **Data dots ($y$):** Actual observed measurements.
- $\text{Residual} = \text{Actual } y - \text{Predicted } \hat{y}$ (dots *above* the line have positive residuals; dots *below* have negative residuals).

#### 4. Two-Way Table Conditional Probability (`Denominator Restriction!`)
Read the phrase right after **"selected at random from..."** or **"given that..."**:
- *"If one item is selected at random from **those with Lead-Free Glaze**, what is the probability it is Porcelain?"*  
  $\implies \text{Denominator} = \mathbf{\text{Total Lead-Free Glaze ONLY}}$ (ignore the grand total of the whole table!).

#### 5. Margin of Error & Causation vs. Association
- **Population Interval from Sample % $\pm$ Margin of Error:** Multiply `(p% - MoE)` and `(p% + MoE)` by the **total target population $N$** (not sample size $n$!).
- **Cause-and-Effect (`"causes / leads to"`):** Requires **random assignment of treatments** (controlled experiment). Surveys with random sampling only establish **correlation/association** within the sampled population.
